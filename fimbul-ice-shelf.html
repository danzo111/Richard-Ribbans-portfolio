Mapping the inside of an ice shelf by drone

Ice shelves crack from underneath, where nobody can see. For my master’s research I flew ground-penetrating radar over Antarctica’s Fimbul Ice Shelf and built a 3D model of what is happening inside it.

My role
Survey planning, drone pilot, processing
When
January 2025 fieldwork
Where
Fimbul Ice Shelf, Antarctica
For
MSc research, University of Cape Town

The problem

The Fimbul Ice Shelf is cut by crevasses that open from its base and climb towards the surface. How far they have climbed matters for how stable the shelf is, but they are hidden under snow and can’t be seen from above.

The usual ways of looking both fall short. Towing a radar across the surface is slow and puts people on crevassed ice. Satellite radar is safe, but too coarse to show the shape of the base. The features I was after had not been mapped since 2011.

After a scoping review of how ice shelves are surveyed, I settled on a third option: carry the radar on a drone, low and slow, so nobody has to walk the line.

80 – 89% of the ice shelf’s thickness is already cut through by basal crevasses at its western calving face. That is the headline result of the 3D model.

Planning the campaign

The fieldwork was part of the SAAS 2024/25 Antarctic Field Campaign. Before leaving I drew the field operations map for the team: the 21 × 6 km working area, known and suspected crevasses, the survey blocks, the supply route back to base and the coordinates of every planned instrument site.

The equipment

  • Aircraft: DJI Matrice 350 RTK
  • Radar: Zond Aero LF ground-penetrating radar at 100 MHz, a low frequency chosen to reach deep into the ice
  • Positioning: Trimble R4S GNSS receivers for ground control
  • Flight profile: 5 m above the surface at 4 m/s

Flying the lines

I flew every survey line as pilot in command, within visual line of sight. The flights started with system tests, then moved on to transects along and across the shelf, a line targeting a known crevasse, and a grid over the western block.

From radar to model

Raw radargrams are noisy, and the layering in this ice is fine and dense. I tested how best to clean the data, found that the standard feature-detection methods couldn’t cope, and developed two extraction methods of my own. The result was a set of georeferenced point clouds that I assembled into a 3D model of the shelf.

The processing has its own write-up: From radargram to 3D model.

What the model shows

At the western calving face, basal crevasses have propagated through 80 to 89% of the total ice thickness. That puts a number on a structural risk to the shelf, and gives later surveys a baseline to measure change against.

What I’d do next

  • Use variable permittivity profiles through the ice instead of a single value, to tighten the depth estimates.
  • Feed the drone’s pitch, roll and heading into the processing, so the radar’s pointing direction is accounted for.
