From radargram to 3D model

A radar survey is only as useful as its processing. This is the pipeline I built to turn raw drone radar data into a georeferenced 3D model, and the two extraction methods I had to develop along the way.

Input
100 MHz drone radargrams
Output
Georeferenced point clouds and a 3D model
Developed for
Fimbul Ice Shelf survey
New methods
Layer and crevasse-tip extraction

The pipeline at a glance

Six stages take the data from the aircraft to the model. Each one is described below.

  1. AcquireLow-level radar lines flown by drone
  2. PositionEvery trace tied to GNSS coordinates
  3. DenoiseBM3D filtering that keeps fine layering
  4. ExtractLayers and crevasse tips picked out
  5. GeoreferenceFeatures become 3D point clouds
  6. ModelPoint clouds assembled into a 3D model

1. Acquire

The radar is a Zond Aero LF at 100 MHz, carried under a DJI Matrice 350 RTK. I flew the lines 5 m above the surface at 4 m/s. Low and slow gives dense, consistent traces, and the low frequency reaches deep enough to see the base of the ice.

2. Position

A radargram on its own is a picture with no address. Each trace is tied to its position along the flight line, with Trimble R4S GNSS receivers providing control on the ground, so everything extracted later lands in real-world coordinates.

3. Denoise

Raw radargrams carry noise that hides the faint reflections from internal layers. I tested enhancement techniques and validated block-matching and 3D filtering (BM3D) for this data: it reduces the noise while preserving the fine layering that the next step depends on.

4. Extract the features

This was the hard part. I first assessed the conventional feature-extraction techniques and found they were not adequate for layering this fine and this dense. So I developed two methods for the job.

The first traces the near-horizontal layers inside the ice. The second finds the tips of crevasses rising from the base. The schematic below shows what they pick out: move across it to fly the radar and see what it would report.

5. Georeference

Each extracted feature is converted from a position in the radargram to a point in space. The output is a set of georeferenced point clouds: one family of points for the internal layers, another for the crevasse tips.

6. Model

The point clouds are assembled into a 3D model of the ice shelf’s internal and external geometry. On the Fimbul Ice Shelf, the model showed basal crevasses reaching 80 to 89% of the way through the ice at the western calving face.

Limits and next steps

  • Depth depends on how fast the radar signal travels through the ice. The next version should use variable permittivity profiles instead of a single value.
  • The drone’s pitch, roll and heading should be included as variables, so the antenna’s pointing direction is corrected for.

Using this on your data

The pipeline was built for ice, but the stages are the same wherever radar is used to look below a surface. If you have radargrams that need cleaning up, features that need picking, or a radar survey that still needs planning, get in touch.
